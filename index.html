Introduction: Dial-Up, Bookstores, and the “Information Superhighway”

In the mid-1990s, “going online” meant listening to a modem screech and hoping nobody picked up the phone. Web pages were built with bright backgrounds, chunky buttons, and animated GIFs. Yet behind the blinking “Under Construction” signs, something huge was taking shape: a new way to buy things, starting with books.

Amazon.com launched to the public in 1995 as an online bookstore. Jeff Bezos’s idea was simple and ambitious at the same time: sell “every book ever printed” through a website. Instead of wandering shelves in a physical store, readers would browse database-driven lists, type in credit card numbers, and wait for brown cardboard boxes to arrive at their door. What looked like a niche experiment quickly became one of the most visible examples of how the internet could transform everyday life.

This site explores that transformation from three angles:

  • Amazon as the self-proclaimed “Earth’s Biggest Bookstore.”
  • Bookstore wars between chains, independent shops, and online sellers.
  • Competing models of e-commerce, especially Amazon versus eBay, and how peer-to-peer (P2P) networks complicated everything.

Drawing on 1990s journalism, later scholarly research on Amazon and eBay, and studies of peer-to-peer file sharing, the project asks a central question of 1990s internet culture: When shopping and media move online, what do we gain—and what do we risk losing?

Amazon as “Earth’s Biggest Bookstore”

Amazon’s origin story is now mythologized: a 1994 startup in a garage, a quirky name, and a decision to sell books first. Histories of Amazon note that books were attractive for technical and economic reasons: they are standardized, easy to ship, and already organized by ISBN numbers, making them ideal for an early database-driven store.

Early coverage emphasized Amazon’s scale. A typical physical bookstore might carry 50,000–100,000 titles. Amazon’s catalog rapidly expanded into the millions, including obscure academic titles, out-of-print works through partners, and books that most local shops would never stock. A 1997 Wired piece described how Amazon “upstaged” Barnes & Noble by offering deep discounts and a far larger inventory than the chain’s still-developing website, framing Amazon as a scrappy tech company taking on slow, old-fashioned retailers.

Scholars later pointed out that Amazon was not just selling books—it was quietly prototyping a toolkit for digital retail. Features that seem basic now, like automated recommendations and customer reviews, were novel in the 1990s. Sandeep Krishnamurthy’s comparative analysis of Amazon and eBay highlights how Amazon’s “electronic storefront” model centralized control over inventory, logistics, and the customer interface, allowing the company to shape the entire buying experience instead of just hosting listings.

These experiments laid the foundation for Amazon’s later evolution into an “everything store.” But in the 1990s, its public image was still tightly tied to books. That made Amazon feel less threatening—almost literary—at the same time that it was destabilizing the economics of the book trade.

Bookstore Wars: Chains, Indies, and Online Threats

By the time Amazon appeared, the American bookstore landscape had already changed dramatically. Big-box chains like Barnes & Noble and Borders were spreading across suburban malls and city corners, offering huge inventories, comfy chairs, and in-store cafés. For many independent bookstores, the arrival of a chain store on their block felt like an early signal that they were doomed.

Amazon layered a new kind of pressure on top of that. Online bookstores did not pay high urban rents, did not close at 9 p.m., and did not run out of shelf space. As Amazon’s sales volume grew, it could negotiate steeper discounts from publishers and pass some of those savings to readers. Commentators in the late 1990s warned that independent shops and even national chains might not survive a world where customers could comparison-shop from their bedrooms 24/7.

Harvard Business School scholar Ryan Raffaelli later described this period as a “near-death experience” for independent bookstores. His research shows that the number of U.S. indie bookstores fell sharply after Amazon’s 1995 launch, confirming what many owners felt on the ground: the internet and the superstores together had changed the rules of the game.

The story does not end there, however. Raffaelli and others argue that from roughly 2009 to 2018, indie bookstores staged a surprising resurgence by leaning into the things Amazon could not easily replicate: hyper-local community, curated selections instead of endless choice, and events that turned stores into social and cultural hubs. Marketing case studies describe this strategy as the “three C’s”—community, curation, and convening—and suggest that bookstores rebranded themselves less as retail outlets and more as neighborhood institutions.

In that sense, the battle between Amazon and bookstores was never just about prices or inventory. It was about whether book buying would be treated as a purely transactional, logistics-driven activity or as something tied to place, conversation, and local identity.

Amazon vs eBay: Two Competing Models of E-Commerce

While Amazon was building a centralized online bookstore, eBay (founded in 1995) developed a very different vision of internet commerce. Instead of acting like a traditional retailer, eBay positioned itself as an auction platform where individuals and small businesses listed items, set starting bids, and relied on other users to drive up prices.

Krishnamurthy’s analysis of the two companies treats them as alternative “archetypes” of the digital economy. Amazon followed a storefront model: it owned or tightly controlled inventory, warehousing, payment processing, and the interface through which customers browsed. eBay followed a marketplace model: it provided rules, feedback systems, and search tools, while shipping and product quality were largely left to individual users.

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, eBay was sometimes seen as the more “pure” internet company because it had little physical infrastructure and generated high margins from fees. Amazon, by contrast, was famous for losses and razor-thin profits, as it poured money into warehouses and logistics. Investors debated which model would ultimately define online retail.

Looking back from the 2020s, it is clear that Amazon’s high-infrastructure path won the mainstream. As consumer trust in online shopping grew, Amazon expanded from books into CDs, DVDs, electronics, toys, and eventually almost every retail category. eBay remained important—especially for used goods, collectibles, and global person-to-person trade—but it no longer set the tone for how most people expected online shopping to feel.

The Amazon–eBay contrast shows that the future of e-commerce was not predetermined. Different models competed, and the one that centralized logistics, data, and customer attention ended up shaping the modern internet more strongly than the open marketplace model that eBay championed.

P2P Issues in the 1990s: Sharing, Piracy, and Platform Control

While companies like Amazon and eBay were inventing ways to sell things online, another force was reshaping digital culture from below: peer-to-peer (P2P) file sharing. In 1999, Shawn Fanning’s software Napster made it easy for millions of users to share MP3 music files directly from their hard drives, bypassing record stores and formal online shops entirely.

P2P systems challenged more than just the music industry’s business model. They raised legal questions about copyright, ethical questions about paying artists, and technical questions about how decentralized networks should be regulated. Studies of the period argue that Napster and its successors created measurable pressure on CD sales, but also introduced audiences to new artists and foreshadowed the later shift toward legal streaming.

For companies like Amazon and eBay, P2P was both a threat and a warning. If users could easily share digital files (music, books, software) with each other for free, then the idea of selling individual copies online started to look fragile. Legal battles in the early 2000s—such as the Napster shutdown and later cases targeting other P2P networks and college campuses—show how intensely media industries and courts fought to re-assert control.

At the same time, P2P technologies influenced expectations about what online systems should do. Users grew accustomed to instant access, massive catalogs, and the feeling that “everything is available somewhere.” In response, mainstream platforms like Amazon invested heavily in making their legal services nearly as convenient as the unauthorized alternatives—one-click purchasing, fast shipping, and later, digital downloads and streaming.

The tension between centralized platforms and decentralized sharing remains a core question in digital culture. The 1990s P2P battles foreshadow later fights over torrents, streaming sites, and even questions about who controls data on social media platforms.

Conclusion: From Corner Stores to Cloud Platforms

In the 1990s, Amazon’s online bookstore looked like one more quirky dot-com experiment. Today, its early focus on books is recognized as the starting point for a much larger transformation of retail, logistics, and media distribution. Independent bookstores and chain stores had to reinvent themselves or vanish. eBay showed that users could trade directly with one another, while P2P networks showed that they could sometimes bypass markets altogether.

The story is not simply about one company winning. It is about a shift from local, place-based commerce toward globally networked platforms that blend data, logistics, and algorithmic personalization. By examining Amazon, eBay, bookstores, and P2P in the context of 1990s internet culture, we can see how our current digital world emerged from specific technological choices and business models—not inevitable progress.

For a comparison with the present day—when Amazon operates cloud infrastructures, e-book ecosystems, and same-day delivery, and when eBay serves niche collectors around the world—see the companion page “From Bookstore to Global Platform: Amazon, eBay, and Online Life Today.”
