Introduction: From Dial-Up Experiments to Everyday Infrastructure

In the 1990s, Amazon and eBay looked like daring experiments on the edge of the “information superhighway.” Today, they feel like background infrastructure. Many shoppers start their product searches directly on Amazon rather than on a search engine. eBay quietly moves billions of dollars of goods each year in global collector and resale markets. Smartphones, recommendation algorithms, and frictionless payment systems make online buying almost invisible.

This companion page compares that present reality with the 1990s origins outlined on the main site. It traces how Amazon grew from a focused online bookstore into a diversified technology conglomerate and how eBay shifted from headline-grabbing auctions to a more specialized, but still significant, marketplace.

Amazon’s Growth: From Books to “Everything” (and the Cloud)

Historical timelines of Amazon emphasize a series of key milestones: the company’s 1997 IPO; the launch of Amazon Marketplace in 1999, which allowed third-party sellers to list items alongside Amazon’s own inventory; the introduction of Amazon Prime in 2005; and the rise of Amazon Web Services (AWS), which now powers large portions of the modern internet.

Analyses of Amazon’s evolution argue that the company’s real breakthrough was combining retail logistics with data and cloud computing. As more customers used the site, Amazon accumulated detailed information on browsing, purchasing, and reviewing patterns. That data fed back into recommendation systems, pricing algorithms, and decisions about which product categories to enter next.

Scholars of publishing highlight how Amazon leveraged its dominance in print book sales to shape the e-book market. By tightly integrating the Kindle device, its proprietary e-book format, and its storefront, Amazon made digital reading convenient while also centralizing control over pricing and access.

Today, discussions of Amazon often revolve around questions of market power, labor conditions in warehouses, and the impact of its logistics network on local retail and the environment. But those debates are rooted in the mid-1990s decision to treat books as the starting point for a much larger experiment in how to organize online commerce.

eBay Today: From Wild Auctions to Curated Resale

eBay’s path since the 1990s has been less dramatic than Amazon’s but still revealing. Business research on the company notes that eBay gradually shifted away from pure auctions toward fixed-price listings (“Buy It Now”) and professional sellers. At the same time, it remained a crucial site for cross-border resale, collectibles, and niche items that are difficult to find in mainstream retail channels.

Comparative studies highlight that eBay never built the same vertically integrated logistics system that Amazon did. Instead, it refined its reputation mechanisms, search tools, and seller policies, trying to maintain trust in a marketplace where it did not physically touch the goods. Krishnamurthy’s early 2000s analysis of eBay’s model still resonates in the 2020s: eBay monetizes information and interactions more than warehouses and delivery trucks.

In the current era of climate concerns and interest in circular economies, eBay’s role in keeping goods in circulation has taken on new meaning. Scholars of sustainability point to resale platforms as part of a broader ecosystem that includes thrift stores, local book and record shops, and clothing resale apps.

From Websites to Platforms: Everything (and Everyone) Online

In the 1990s, Amazon and eBay were “websites” that people visited through desktop browsers. Today, they function as multi-sided platforms integrated into app ecosystems, voice assistants, smart TVs, and even physical devices. AWS underpins many other companies’ services, while Amazon’s logistics network sets expectations for speed across the entire retail sector.

At the same time, some patterns from the 1990s have come full circle. The renewed strength of independent bookstores, often using online tools like bookshop.org and social media to coordinate with readers, suggests that hybrid models can thrive alongside dominant platforms.

The legacy of P2P file sharing is also visible in the streaming era. Legal services like Spotify, Netflix, and Kindle Unlimited offer enormous libraries in exchange for recurring payments and heavy data collection. The tension between centralized convenience and decentralized control that began with Napster now shapes debates over platform monopolies, data rights, and algorithmic recommendation across the whole digital economy.

What started as “an online bookstore” and “an online auction site” have thus become case studies in how infrastructure, data, and governance come together to define everyday online life.
